Clear Choice

Transparent and controllable recommendations in a shopping assistant

Abstract 181 words; main including headings 1005; total 1186.


Abstract


Conversational recommendations can conceal both commercial influence and the use of personal information. Building on the A1 literature review, Clear Choice explores how a shopping assistant can distinguish sponsorship, explain the information behind a recommendation, and make user controls demonstrably effective. The developed browser prototype uses six fictional headphone products and a deterministic recommendation engine. Users state a budget and purpose, inspect ordinary and sponsored results, and open explanations derived from the inputs actually used. Purpose-based advertising personalisation is off by default; users can enable it, withdraw it, hide sponsored placements, undo changes, or reset the session. Budget remains an explicit constraint on every result. The limited conversation and commercial environment simulate an AI shopping context; no live language model, advertiser, payment or external tracking is connected. Fifteen automated checks and a browser walkthrough verify key control and explanation behaviors, including withdrawal, unchanged ordinary rankings, ambiguous requests and empty results. These checks demonstrate implementation behavior, not improved human understanding. A future study would compare sponsorship labels alone with input-specific explanations and assess comprehension and preference-consistent control rather than increased trust alone.


Problem and project scope


A1 identified three distinct difficulties: recognising sponsorship, understanding personalisation, and exercising effective control. Clear Choice develops that opportunity into a bounded headphone-shopping interaction. Tang et al. (2025) found difficulties detecting conversational advertisements and observed attempts to question or control them through dialogue. This motivates visible commercial labels alongside conversational controls; it does not establish that this prototype's design will improve understanding.

The developed scope is one session, six fictional products, one maximum budget and one stated purpose. Implemented behavior includes parsing supported phrases, filtering, ranking, explaining, changing settings, undo and export. Sponsorship and open-ended AI dialogue are simulated. Live LLM interpretation, real advertising infrastructure and a comprehension study remain planned. This scope makes information use inspectable without claiming to reproduce a commercial platform.


Design rationale


First, ordinary recommendations and a sponsored placement occupy separate regions. Each sponsored card identifies its fictional sponsor; ordinary cards state that they are not sponsored. This applies visibility of system status: commercial influence is available at the point of recommendation. Separating the rankings also supports an accurate mental model. Turning advertising off removes its placement without changing ordinary ranking, rather than implying that payment makes a product objectively better.

Second, explanations disclose specific inputs and their effects. Kim et al. (2019) distinguish acceptable and objectionable information flows, qualifying the assumption that transparency should increase trust. Accordingly, the objective is informed choice. The explanation distinguishes the headphone category, the affordability constraint and optional purpose-based ranking. Lee et al. (2023) show that explanation needs vary with concerns about information collection and advertiser motives. Details therefore open on demand, while sponsorship remains visible without an extra click.

Third, purpose use for advertising requires an explicit opt-in, separate from its use for ordinary shopping assistance. The budget always applies and is described as such. This prevents a privacy switch from silently allowing unaffordable recommendations. Ngong et al. (2025) motivate attention to contextual information use, although their intervention does not validate this advertising design. The prototype collects no inferred demographics or external browsing history and keeps session information in browser memory.

Fourth, control is coupled to execution and feedback. Disabling purpose use recomputes sponsored ranking and removes purpose from its explanation metadata. Hiding advertising removes the placement. This responds to Castleman and Korolova's (2024) finding that a tested advertising control did not significantly reduce the selected topics. Their result concerns Meta, not this prototype; it motivates checking effects rather than trusting reassuring messages. A revision indicator, current-data panel and undo operation apply feedback, user control and reversibility to make changes observable and repairable.

Finally, the interaction preserves correct context while repairing uncertainty. A new budget retains the current purpose. Multiple purposes, conflicting budgets and ambiguous control requests prompt clarification without changing state. This applies interaction repair: ask for the missing decision rather than restart the task or guess. When no product fits, the system retains the budget and presents an empty result. Avoiding an unsupported recommendation is an intentional response, not an execution failure.

The controls deliberately distinguish stopping a use from deleting information. Withdrawing advertising permission leaves the stated purpose available for ordinary recommendations, and earlier conversation messages remain visible. Reset clears the current session, while an already downloaded evidence file remains on the device. Describing these boundaries prevents a misleading mental model of deletion. A stronger deletion workflow would require its own design and verification rather than a reassuring label.


System and illustrated interaction


Figure 1 separates the implemented browser pipeline from simulated commercial data and planned model interpretation. A small rule-based parser converts supported phrases into events; validated state feeds two ranking paths. Prices above budget are excluded. Ordinary ranking rewards purpose matches and then battery life. Sponsored ranking uses purpose only when enabled, followed by fixed campaign priority. Explanations use the same result metadata, preventing a separate generator from inventing reasons. There is no trained-model inference or autonomous tool loop.

The storyboards show three developed interactions: recognising and inspecting a recommendation, withdrawing data use, and recovering from an empty result. They illustrate actual browser-checked states. For a commuting request under £100, enabling purpose use changes the sponsor selection from Aster Lite to Metro Quiet; withdrawing it restores Aster Lite. This visible change is illustrative evidence, while input-exclusion tests check the underlying rule.


Development evidence and evaluation


Lab 4 informed provenance display, explicit control and refusal patterns. The A2 implementation is newly generated JavaScript; the lab's Python application and model mocks were not copied. Lab 5 supplied development methods: bounded tasks, repository instructions, a reusable verification skill, tests, trace and handover. Its starter remains separate and is not represented as an A2 feature. The user accepted the project direction; Codex authored the implementation, tests and report draft. Independent student review is still required.

Fifteen engine tests passed. They cover opt-in and withdrawal, explanation-input consistency, invariant ordinary results, ambiguous requests, and a budget sweep from £1 to £1,000. An initial failure came from a test fixture that modified ordinary products while testing sponsored priorities; correcting that fixture retained the assertion. Browser checks confirmed consent changes, explanation, withdrawal, hiding, undo, empty results, clarification, export and reset. These observations and the actual session export are retained in the development record.

The main limitation is that deterministic consistency does not establish human comprehension. No participant study or improvement in trust is claimed. Future evaluation should compare label-only and input-specific explanations, ask users which information affected selection, and check whether they can enact their preferred data-use settings. Completion time and effort should be measured alongside accuracy. The planned live-model extension would need separate evaluation of extraction errors and faithful explanations before replacing the bounded parser.

A significant scope compromise is the absence of a live language model. A fluent shopping assistant could accept more varied requests, but its explanations might not faithfully describe targeting. The bounded parser and shared execution metadata prioritise inspectability for the A1 question. This version can support a walkthrough of control behavior, but cannot answer whether the same interface remains understandable in a long, unpredictable shopping conversation.
